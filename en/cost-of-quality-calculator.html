What Is Cost of Quality (CoQ)?

Why break quality cost into four categories?

Quality cost splits into "prevention and appraisal" (proactive investment) and "internal and external failure" (reactive cleanup). Money spent on prevention and appraisal keeps defects from happening or catches them early; failure costs are what you pay for defects that already occurred. The ratio between the two groups reveals whether your company manages quality proactively or is mostly firefighting after the fact.

How should I use the result?

If internal and external failure costs are much larger than prevention and appraisal, increasing prevention investment often reduces total cost over time — one of the core principles of quality management. Frequently Asked Questions

What's the difference between prevention and appraisal cost?

Prevention cost is investment (training, process design, preventive maintenance) that keeps defects from occurring at all. Appraisal cost is the inspection and measurement spent finding defects in products that already exist. Both happen "before" the customer sees the product, but prevention removes the cause while appraisal filters the output.

Why is external failure cost considered the riskiest category?

Internal failures are contained inside the company, but external failures can damage customer trust, harm brand reputation, and in serious cases create legal liability — so the real impact can be far larger than the visible dollar figure.

How can I reduce cost of quality?

Increasing investment in prevention and appraisal typically reduces internal and external failure costs by a larger multiple. That said, this is a general tendency — the actual effect varies by industry and process, so it's important to validate against your own data.

Cost of Quality (COQ) Guide: Prevention, Appraisal and Failure Costs and Their Improvement Effect

Cost of quality (COQ) is the cost of achieving quality plus the loss from poor quality. It splits into prevention, appraisal, internal failure and external failure costs. Spending more on prevention tends to reduce failure costs, so separating them shows where to invest. This guide covers the categories, how to calculate them and how to use the result.

Four categories

CategoryContentExamples
PreventionActivities that stop defects occurringTraining, process design, supplier evaluation, preventive maintenance
AppraisalActivities that verify qualityIncoming and in-process inspection, testing, calibration
Internal failureLoss from defects found before shipmentScrap, rework, re-inspection, downtime
External failureLoss from defects found by customersReturns, claims, warranty repair, lost trust

How to calculate

  1. Assign cost items to each category.
  2. Total each category for the period.
  3. Express each as a share of sales.
  4. Find the largest failure category and analyze causes.
  5. Estimate how much failure cost falls if prevention and appraisal spending rise.

With sales of 1,000 million, prevention of 10 million, appraisal of 20 million, internal failure of 30 million and external failure of 40 million, COQ is 100 million, or 10% of sales. The figures are illustrative.

Cautions

  • External failure costs are understated by visible claims because loss of trust is also a cost.
  • Prevention pays back slowly, so judge it on a trend.
  • Use one definition across the company so that comparisons make sense.
  • The goal is to reduce the cost of poor quality, not quality activity itself.
