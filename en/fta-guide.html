Self-Certification vs Authority-Issued Certification

Self-Certification

  • The exporter, producer, or (in some agreements) the importer prepares and signs the Certificate of Origin directly — no third-party stamp required
  • Used in agreements such as USMCA, CPTPP, and most EU trade agreements
  • Faster to issue, but the certifier carries the burden of proof if customs later audits the origin claim
  • Careful review of the origin criteria before signing is essential — an incorrect claim can mean back duties and penalties

Authority-Issued Certification

  • Issued by a government body or an authorized chamber of commerce after reviewing supporting documents
  • Used in agreements such as the ASEAN Trade in Goods Agreement (Form D) and many bilateral agreements across Asia
  • More readily accepted by the importing country's customs authority, but takes longer to obtain
  • Requires supporting documentation (bill of materials, cost breakdown) to be submitted to the issuing authority

Pre-Issuance Checklist

  • Have you confirmed the correct HS code for the exported product?
  • Does the product meet the agreement's rules of origin (wholly obtained, tariff shift, or value-added/regional value content threshold)?
  • Have you prepared supporting documentation (bill of materials, cost breakdown) in case of an origin audit?
  • If self-certifying, have you confirmed whether "approved exporter" or "Registered Exporter (REX)" status is required?
  • Have you compared the preferential tariff rate against the standard (MFN) rate to confirm it's actually worth claiming?
  • Will the Certificate of Origin reach the importer within its validity period (commonly around 12 months, but this varies by agreement)?

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the practical difference between self-certification and authority-issued Certificates of Origin?

Self-certification lets the exporter prepare and sign the C/O directly, which is faster but puts the burden of proof on the exporter if customs later audits the claim. Authority-issued certification involves a government body or chamber of commerce reviewing documents before issuing the C/O — slower, but generally more readily accepted by the importing customs authority.

How long is a Certificate of Origin typically valid?

It varies by agreement, but 12 months from the issue date is common. Always confirm the specific validity period for the agreement you're using, and plan issuance timing so the C/O reaches the importer while still valid.

FTA Certificate of Origin Guide: Origin Criteria, Proof of Origin and How to Claim Preferential Duty

A free trade agreement can cut import duty below the normal rate, sometimes to zero. Buying from a partner country does not make that happen by itself: the goods must meet the agreement’s rules of origin and you must hold proof of origin. This guide explains the types of origin criteria, how proof of origin is prepared, the order of steps for claiming preference and the mistakes that cost importers their benefit.

Conditions for claiming FTA preference

  1. The agreement is in force and the goods’ tariff line has a preferential rate.
  2. The goods qualify as originating under the agreement; shipping from a partner country is not enough.
  3. You hold valid proof of origin, whether a certificate or an origin declaration.
  4. Transport conditions are met. Goods generally must travel directly between the parties, with limited exceptions for transit.
  5. Preference is claimed when the import declaration is filed.

Three kinds of origin criteria

TypeMeaningExample
Wholly obtainedEntirely produced or obtained in the partyCrops, minerals, fish

Regional value content (RVC)A minimum share of value added in the regionAssembled or processed goods
Specific processA named process must be performedSome textile and chemical goods

Which test applies is set per HS line in the agreement’s product-specific rules. Calculate value content with the RVC calculator.

How proof of origin is prepared

  • Authority-issued certificate: a designated body in the exporting country issues the certificate.
  • Self-certification: some agreements allow approved exporters, or exporters and producers, to make an origin declaration.
  • Importer’s knowledge: a few agreements let the importer claim preference based on its own information.
  • A document normally names the goods, tariff code, origin criterion, exporter and importer and the date of issue. Validity is set by each agreement, commonly about one year, so check the text.

Steps to claim preference

  1. Confirm the HS code (HS code guide).
  2. Look up the preferential rate and the agreements that apply in the official tariff database.
  3. Ask the supplier whether the goods meet the origin criterion and how proof will be issued.
  4. Write the proof-of-origin obligation into the purchase contract.
  5. Claim preference at declaration and keep the document.
  6. Keep supporting records, such as bills of materials and process data, in case of post-clearance verification.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming purchase or shipment from a partner country is enough.
  • Claiming after the proof of origin has expired.
  • Product name or tariff code on the invoice differs from the certificate.
  • Trans-shipping through a non-party and breaking the direct transport rule.
  • Not keeping records that would support a verification request.
  • Not comparing the best rate where several agreements apply.

What else to check

Preference covers duty only. VAT and excise taxes still apply. Run both the preferential and the general rate through the landed cost calculator to see the true saving. Always confirm current rates and rules on the official sites.
