Why CBM & Volumetric Weight Matter

Carriers bill whichever is higher: your shipment's actual weight, or its volumetric (dimensional) weight. Light but bulky cargo — think foam displays, inflatables, or bulky packaging — can cost far more than its actual weight suggests once volumetric weight is applied. Estimating CBM before you book helps you avoid surprise freight bills and pick the right container size.

How to use it in practice: Before confirming a booking, enter every carton size in your shipment (mixed cartons are supported) to see total CBM, both sea and air chargeable weight, and how many 20ft/40ft containers you'd need. If air freight chargeable weight is much higher than actual weight, ask your forwarder whether sea freight is more economical for this shipment.

Frequently Asked Questions

What exactly is CBM and how is it calculated?

CBM (Cubic Meter) measures the volume of a shipment in cubic meters. Multiply length x width x height (in meters) for one carton, then multiply by quantity. For example, 100 cartons at 50x40x30cm each equals 0.5 x 0.4 x 0.3 x 100 = 6 CBM.

Why do sea freight and air freight use different volumetric weight formulas?

Carriers price cargo based on how dense it is relative to their vehicle's capacity. Sea freight conventionally uses CBM x 1,000 kg/m3 (roughly 1 CBM = 1 ton), while air freight uses the IATA standard of volume (cm3) divided by 6,000. The same shipment can have a very different chargeable weight depending on the mode of transport.

How much can actually fit in a 20ft vs 40ft container?

Theoretical internal capacity is about 33 m3 for a 20ft container and 67 m3 for a 40ft container, but in practice only around 85-92% of that is usable due to pallet gaps and irregular cargo shapes. A safer planning figure is roughly 28 m3 for 20ft and 58 m3 for 40ft.

How is chargeable weight different from actual weight?

Chargeable weight is whichever is greater: actual weight or volumetric weight. Bulky but lightweight cargo (like products with a lot of padding) often has a higher volumetric weight than actual weight, which can make freight cost more than you'd expect from the physical weight alone.

CBM Calculation Guide: Formula, LCL Freight, Air Volumetric Weight and Container Loading

CBM (cubic metre) is the unit that describes how much space a shipment occupies, and it drives ocean freight pricing and container planning. This guide covers the CBM formula, how LCL freight is charged, how air volumetric weight works, typical 20ft and 40ft container capacities, and the mistakes importers make most often. The CBM formula

For a rectangular package, CBM is length × width × height in metres. If you measured in centimetres, multiply the three values and divide by 1,000,000.

  • CBM = L(m) × W(m) × H(m)
  • CBM = L(cm) × W(cm) × H(cm) ÷ 1,000,000
  • For several identical cartons, multiply the CBM of one carton by the quantity.
  • If you measured in inches, divide cubic inches by 61,024 to get CBM.

A 60 × 40 × 40 cm carton is 0.096 CBM, so 50 cartons come to 4.8 CBM. Always measure the outer dimensions of the shipping pack (carton plus pallet if used), not the product itself.

How LCL ocean freight is charged

Shipments that do not fill a container travel as LCL (less than container load). LCL freight is normally charged on whichever is greater, weight or volume, a basis the trade calls W/M (weight or measurement). Carriers and forwarders commonly treat 1 CBM as equal to 1,000 kg, one revenue ton, and apply the rate to the larger figure.

Light, bulky cargo is therefore priced on CBM and heavy, compact cargo on tonnage. Minimum charges and the exact basis are stated in your forwarder’s quotation, so check them before you commit.

Air freight volumetric weight

Air cargo is charged on chargeable weight, the greater of actual weight and volumetric weight. The standard IATA conversion is 6,000 cubic centimetres per kilogram.

  • Volumetric weight (kg) = L(cm) × W(cm) × H(cm) ÷ 6,000
  • Express couriers such as DHL, FedEx and UPS often use a divisor of 5,000, which produces a higher chargeable weight for the same box.
  • Calculate each carton separately, add them up, and expect rounding up to the next 0.5 kg.

The 60 × 40 × 40 cm carton weighing 15 kg has a volumetric weight of 16 kg, higher than its actual weight, so 50 cartons by air are charged at 800 kg.

Container capacity guide

External dimensions and ratings of freight containers are defined in ISO 668, while usable internal volume varies slightly by manufacturer and carrier. These are typical working figures.

ContainerInternal volume (approx.)Practical loading
20 ft (20GP)about 33 CBMabout 25–28 CBM
40 ft (40GP)about 67 CBMabout 55–60 CBM
40 ft high cube (40HC)about 76 CBMabout 62–68 CBM

Practical loading is lower than internal volume because of gaps between cartons, pallet sizes, door clearance and weight limits. Heavy cargo can reach the weight limit long before the container is full, so check both CBM and gross weight.

Pallets and packaging change your CBM

The same goods can have a different CBM and freight cost depending on how they are packed. Palletizing speeds handling but adds the pallet’s own height and footprint. The pallet widely used in Europe is 1,200 × 800 mm, and the ISO pallet is 1,200 × 1,000 mm.

  • Adjust carton sizes to the pallet footprint to reduce empty space.
  • Trimming carton height by 1–2 cm can noticeably cut total CBM on orders of several hundred cartons.
  • Changing inner-pack configuration changes how many units fit in a container.

Common mistakes

  • Mixing centimetres and metres, which makes results off by a factor of 1,000,000.
  • Measuring the product instead of the shipping carton and under-quoting freight.
  • Rounding each carton and then summing; it is more accurate to sum first and round at the end.
  • Checking only ocean volume and forgetting the air volumetric divisor.
  • Looking at container CBM while overlooking the gross weight limit.

More questions about CBM

How many decimals should I keep? It depends on the forwarder, but a common practice is to calculate each line to three decimals, add them up and round the total up at two decimals. Check the rounding rule in your quotation.

What about cylindrical or irregular cargo? Treat it as a rectangular block using its largest length, width and height, including any protruding parts.

Can knock-down or foldable designs cut CBM? Shipping unassembled often reduces CBM and freight markedly, but compare that with assembly cost and quality risks.
