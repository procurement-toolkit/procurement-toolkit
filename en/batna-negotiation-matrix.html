Key Terms

BATNA: Your best alternative if this negotiation fails. A stronger BATNA gives you more negotiating power.

Reservation Price: The point beyond which you're better off walking away and taking your BATNA instead.

ZOPA: The overlap between your reservation price and the counterparty's reservation price. A deal is only structurally possible if this overlap exists.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I estimate the counterparty's range?

Base it on their cost structure, market pricing, past deal history, and competitor quotes. It doesn't need to be precise — even a rough range meaningfully improves your negotiation strategy.

If there's no ZOPA, should I give up on the negotiation?

Not necessarily — first consider whether adjusting non-price terms (lead time, payment terms, volume, warranty) could widen the ZOPA. Bundling multiple issues together can create room for a deal that pure price negotiation couldn't.

Why should my reservation price be based on my BATNA?

The worst deal you should accept can never be worse than your best alternative (BATNA) if the negotiation fails. If a deal is worse than your BATNA, walking away is the rational choice.

BATNA Analysis Guide: How to Prepare and Strengthen Your Procurement Negotiating Position

BATNA, the Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement, is the best option you have if talks fail. The concept was popularized by Roger Fisher and William Ury, and the better your alternative, the stronger your position. In purchasing, alternative suppliers, in-house production and demand changes can all be a BATNA. This guide covers how to define it, compare both sides and use it in negotiation.

Defining your BATNA

  1. List every option you have if the negotiation fails.
  2. Assess cost, risk and time for each.
  3. Select the best one as your BATNA.
  4. Set your walk-away point, the least acceptable deal, on that basis.
  5. Consider improving the alternative, for example by qualifying another supplier.

Comparing both sides

Your sideSupplier side
AlternativesOther suppliers, in-house production, specification changeOther customers, selling from stock, adjusting production
StrengthStronger with more suppliersStronger with more customers
LeverageHigher with a better alternativeHigher with a better alternative

Estimating the other side’s BATNA shows how far they can move. If the supplier depends on one customer, your leverage is high.

Using it in procurement

  • Gather several quotes (see the RFQ comparison tool).
  • If your supplier is the only one, start qualifying alternatives first.
  • Write the walk-away point down so emotion does not move you in the room.
  • Extend negotiation beyond price to delivery, payment terms and volume commitments.
  • Decide carefully whether to reveal your BATNA.

Common mistakes

  • Entering talks without listing alternatives.
  • Underestimating the cost of switching suppliers, such as qualification, tooling and stock.
  • Guessing the other side’s BATNA without checking.
  • Negotiating without a walk-away point and accepting a weak deal.
