Why These Terms Matter

Prime Cost, Manufacturing Cost, and Total Cost cover progressively wider scopes — mixing them up during a cost or margin negotiation can lead to a real pricing error. The same confusion applies to Markup vs Margin: a "30%" markup and a "30%" margin produce different sell prices for the same cost base.

How to use it in practice: If you manufacture, build up from Direct Material and Direct Labor to Manufacturing Overhead and SG&A to see your full Total Cost. If you import/trade, start from Purchase Price and add incidental costs (duty, freight, insurance) to get your Landed/Acquisition Cost before adding SG&A.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the difference between Prime Cost, Manufacturing Cost (COGM), and Total Cost?

Prime Cost includes only direct materials and direct labor. Manufacturing Cost (COGM) adds manufacturing overhead (factory operating costs). Total Cost adds selling and administrative expenses on top of that — it's the full cost of getting a product ready and sold.

Why do Markup and Margin get confused so often?

Markup is profit divided by cost; Margin is profit divided by selling price. The same dollar amount of profit produces two different percentages depending on which base you use, which is a frequent source of miscommunication in quotes and negotiations.

How can I verify a supplier's claimed cost breakdown?

Cross-check material costs against known commodity or market pricing, and compare labor costs to the country's minimum wage or industry benchmarks. If overhead looks unusually high relative to direct costs, ask the supplier to justify that specific line item.

Cost Structure Analysis: Material, Conversion and Overhead Ratios and Break-Even Calculation

Cost structure shows how product cost divides into materials, labour, overhead and administration. Knowing the ratios tells you which item pays most to reduce, where to press in price negotiations and what volume you need to break even. This guide covers cost ratios, fixed versus variable cost and break-even calculations.

Cost ratios

Divide each cost by total cost. With materials of 5,000, labour of 1,500, manufacturing overhead of 1,000 and SG&A of 1,000, total cost is 8,500 and materials are about 58.8%. Material-heavy products depend on commodity prices and yield; labour-heavy products depend on process time and automation.

Variable versus fixed cost

TypeDefinitionExamples
VariableRises with outputMaterials, packaging, freight per shipment
FixedIndependent of outputRent, depreciation, salaried staff
Semi-variablePartly fixed, partly variableElectricity, some labour

Break-even

Break-even quantity = fixed cost ÷ (selling price − variable cost per unit). With fixed cost of 30 million a month, a price of 20,000 and variable cost of 12,000, break-even is 30,000,000 ÷ 8,000 = 3,750 units. With a contribution margin of 40%, break-even revenue is fixed cost ÷ contribution margin.

How to use it

  1. List cost per unit by item.
  2. Split into variable and fixed.
  3. Look for improvement in the largest items.
  4. Set a target margin and calculate the price and volume you need.
  5. Test sensitivity to cost changes.

Common mistakes

  • Spreading fixed cost per unit and keeping it when volume changes.
  • Leaving duty and freight out of import cost (see the landed cost calculator).
  • Ignoring exchange-rate movement.
